---
title: Glossary
---

Each term below has one meaning throughout the notes. In the text, a term with a dotted underline opens its definition.

:::{glossary}
Absolute ceiling
: The altitude at which an aircraft's maximum rate of climb drops to zero, where available thrust equals minimum thrust required (or available power equals minimum power required). An aircraft can approach it only asymptotically.

Aerobraking
: Lowering the apoapsis of an orbit with atmospheric drag instead of a burn: the periapsis is placed in the upper atmosphere, and each pass removes a little speed. A small burn at the end lifts the periapsis out of the atmosphere.

Aerodynamic center
: The point on the chord of an airfoil about which the pitching moment does not change with the angle of attack. On airfoils at low speed it lies near the quarter chord; the moment coefficient about it is written $c_{m,\text{ac}}$.

Afterburner
: A second combustion section placed behind the turbine of a jet engine that burns extra fuel in the oxygen-rich exhaust to boost thrust briefly for takeoff or supersonic combat, at a sharp increase in fuel consumption.

Aileron
: A moving surface on the trailing edge near each wing tip. The two ailerons move in opposite directions and roll the aircraft.

Airfoil
: The cross-section shape of a wing, cut parallel to the centerline: rounded at the leading edge, sharp at the trailing edge, described by its chord, camber, and thickness. Also called a wing section.

Airspeed indicator
: The flight instrument that shows airspeed. A diaphragm inside it has the total pressure on one side and the static pressure on the other, and the dial turns the difference $p_0 - p$ into a speed using the sea-level standard density.

Altimeter
: The flight instrument that shows altitude. Sealed metal capsules (aneroid wafers) inside it expand as the static pressure falls, and its dial shows the standard-atmosphere altitude that has that pressure.

Angle of attack
: The angle $\alpha$ between the free-stream direction and the chord line of an airfoil or wing, positive with the leading edge tilted up into the free stream. It does not depend on the horizon.

Apoapsis
: The point of an orbit farthest from the central body, at radius $r_a = a(1 + e)$. Around Earth it is called the apogee, around the Sun the aphelion.

Area ratio
: The exit area of a nozzle divided by its throat area, $A_e/A_t$; also called the expansion ratio. A larger area ratio expands the gas further, to a lower exit pressure and a higher exit velocity.

Ascending node
: The point where an orbit crosses the reference plane (the equator, for an Earth orbit) going north. The inclination is measured there.

Aspect ratio
: $\textit{AR} = b^2/S$, the wing span squared divided by the wing area. A long, narrow wing has a high aspect ratio; for a rectangular wing it is the span over the chord.

Atomic oxygen
: Oxygen as single atoms, made when ultraviolet sunlight splits oxygen molecules in the thin upper atmosphere. Between about 180 and 650 km it is the most common gas, and at orbital speed it oxidizes and wears away the polymer and composite surfaces of a spacecraft.

Balance
: The instrument a wind-tunnel model is mounted on: a set of load cells that measures the forces (lift, drag, side force) and the moments on the model.

Ballistic coefficient
: $B = m/(C_D A)$, the mass of a vehicle divided by its drag coefficient times its reference area, in kg/m². The higher it is, the less drag slows the vehicle.

Bernoulli's equation
: $p + \tfrac12\rho V^2 + \rho g z$ is the same at every point of a streamline in steady, incompressible flow without friction. Where the fluid speeds up, its pressure falls.

Bipropellant
: A rocket propellant made of two liquids, a fuel and an oxidizer, kept in separate tanks and mixed only in the combustion chamber. Most launch-vehicle engines burn a bipropellant.

Blockage
: The crowding of the flow around a model by the walls of a wind tunnel's test section, measured by the model's frontal area divided by the test-section area. Above about 5% the measured forces need corrections.

Body axes
: Three perpendicular axes fixed to an aircraft and crossing at its center of gravity: $x$ forward through the nose, $y$ out along the right wing, and $z$ down. Roll, pitch, and yaw are rotations about them.

Boil-off
: The loss of a cryogenic propellant that heat leaking into its tank boils away; the vapor is vented to hold the tank pressure. Its rate is the heat leak divided by the heat of vaporization, $\dot Q/h_\text{vap}$.

Boundary layer
: The thin layer of fluid next to a surface in which the speed rises from zero at the wall (no slip) to the speed of the flow outside. Viscous forces matter inside it; outside it the flow behaves as if it had no viscosity.

Breguet range equation
: The weight fraction of a jet's cruise segment, flown in steady level flight at constant speed, specific fuel consumption, and lift-to-drag ratio: $W_i/W_{i-1} = \exp[-R\,C/(V\,(L/D))]$, with the range $R$ in nautical miles, the speed $V$ in knots, and $C$ in 1/h.

Bulk density
: The density of a propellant pair as loaded: the total mass of fuel and oxidizer divided by their total volume. At mixture ratio O/F it is $\rho_\text{bulk} = (1 + \text{O/F})/(\text{O/F}/\rho_\text{ox} + 1/\rho_\text{fuel})$.

Burn time
: $t_b$, the time an engine runs on a load of propellant: the propellant mass divided by the mass flow, $t_b = m_\text{prop}/\dot m$, in s.

Bypass ratio
: The ratio $\beta = \dot{m}_{\text{bypass}}/\dot{m}_{\text{core}}$ of air mass flow that passes through the fan duct around the core of a turbofan to the air mass flow that passes through the gas-turbine core.

Camber
: The distance between the mean camber line of an airfoil and its chord line, usually given at its largest value as a percentage of the chord. A symmetric airfoil has none.

Capture burn
: The burn at the periapsis of an arrival hyperbola, against the direction of motion, that slows a spacecraft enough to stay in orbit around the target. Without it the spacecraft flies past and leaves.

Center of gravity
: The point where the whole weight of a vehicle can be taken to act. The body axes cross there, and the aircraft rotates about it.

Center of pressure
: The point on the chord where the resultant aerodynamic force acts, so that the moment about it is zero. It moves along the chord as the angle of attack changes.

Chamber pressure
: $p_c$, the pressure of the hot gas in the combustion chamber of a rocket engine, in Pa. A higher chamber pressure passes the same mass flow through a smaller throat.

Characteristic velocity
: $c^* = p_c A_t/\dot m$, in m/s: the chamber pressure times the throat area per unit mass flow. A more energetic combustion gives a higher $c^*$, which depends on the propellants and their mixture ratio, not on the nozzle. A higher $c^*$ needs less flow for the same chamber pressure and throat.

Chord
: The distance from the leading edge to the trailing edge of an airfoil, measured along the chord line; $c$. The root chord $c_r$ is at the centerline and the tip chord $c_t$ at the wing tip.

Circular speed
: The speed of a circular orbit of radius $r$: $v_c = \sqrt{\mu/r}$. It falls as the radius grows.

Circularization burn
: A single burn at periapsis or apoapsis that changes the speed to the circular speed at that radius, so the orbit becomes a circle through that point.

Closed-circuit wind tunnel
: A wind tunnel that sends the same air around a closed loop: corners with turning vanes and a return leg bring the air from the diffuser back to the settling chamber, and the fan only makes up for the losses. Also called a Göttingen or closed-return tunnel.

Cold-gas thruster
: A thruster that lets a stored gas, often nitrogen, out through a nozzle with no combustion. It is simple and safe and gives small, precise pushes, at the lowest specific impulse of any rocket.

Combination table
: A table with the functions of a vehicle in rows and several ways to perform each function in columns. Picking one option for each function gives one concept, so the table holds every combination of the options.

Combustion chamber
: The part of a rocket engine where the fuel and the oxidizer burn, giving a hot gas at high pressure that then flows into the nozzle.

Common-cause failure
: The failure of several units from one event or one shared fault, such as the same software error or a shared power supply. It defeats redundancy, which assumes the units fail independently.

Compliance table
: A table that lists every requirement of a system with the method that verifies it (analysis, inspection, demonstration, or test), where the evidence is, and whether the design meets it yet.

Concept
: A short description of how a vehicle will do its job, specific enough to estimate its weight, cost, and performance: for example, a turboprop airplane that takes off from a runway.

Conic section
: A curve cut from a cone by a plane: a circle, an ellipse, a parabola, or a hyperbola. Every trajectory of the two-body problem is one, with the central body at a focus.

Consequence
: How bad the result of a risk would be if its event happened, scored in these notes from 1 (minimal) to 5 (the mission or the program lost).

Constraint
: A limit the designers must respect but did not choose, such as a noise rule set by a regulator. Constraints come from the stakeholders, as needs do.

Constraint diagram
: A plot of sea-level thrust-to-weight ratio $T/W$ against wing loading $W/S$ that draws every performance requirement (stall, runway length, climb, cruise, ceiling) as a boundary curve. The overlap of the feasible sides shows the smallest acceptable wing and engines.

Continuity equation
: Conservation of mass for a steady flow: the mass flow rate $\rho A V$ is the same at every cross section of a duct or a stream tube. For incompressible flow it becomes $A_1 V_1 = A_2 V_2$.

Contraction
: The smoothly narrowing duct of a wind tunnel between the settling chamber and the test section. It speeds the air up and evens out its unsteadiness. Also called the nozzle.

Contraction ratio
: The cross-section area of a wind tunnel's settling chamber divided by that of its test section, $A_1/A_2$. The test-section speed is the settling-chamber speed times this ratio.

Criterion
: A property on which competing concepts are compared in a decision matrix, such as payload and range or operating cost. Criteria (the plural) come from the requirements, and each one measures something the others do not.

Cryogenic propellant
: A liquefied gas that boils below −146 °C at sea-level pressure, such as liquid oxygen, liquid hydrogen, or liquid methane. It must be kept cold, and some of it boils away while it waits.

Decision matrix
: A table that compares concepts, with the criteria in rows and the concepts in columns. Each concept is scored on each criterion, usually from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent), and its weighted total is the sum of weight times score.

Delta-V
: The magnitude of the change of a spacecraft's velocity vector made by a burn, written $\Delta V$ and given in km/s. Through the rocket equation it sets the propellant the burn uses.

Delta-V budget
: The total velocity change a launch vehicle must produce to reach its target orbit: the orbit speed plus the gravity, drag, and steering losses, minus the speed gained from Earth's rotation.

Density
: The mass of a substance per unit volume, $\rho = dm/dv$, in kg/m³. For air it follows from pressure and temperature through the equation of state; at sea level in the standard atmosphere it is 1.2250 kg/m³.

Density altitude
: The altitude in the standard atmosphere at which the density equals the actual density of the air. On a hot day it is higher than the true altitude, and an aircraft performs as it would at the density altitude.

Departure burn
: The burn in a parking orbit that raises a spacecraft's speed to the speed of a departure hyperbola, so it leaves the planet with the hyperbolic excess speed it needs.

Diffuser
: The long, slowly widening duct of a wind tunnel downstream of the test section. It slows the air and raises its static pressure again; losses keep it from recovering all of it.

Dihedral
: The upward angle of a wing from the root to the tip, seen from the front. It makes the aircraft tend to roll its wings level after a gust.

Downwash
: The downward velocity $w$ that the wingtip vortices give the air at and behind a lifting wing. It tilts the flow the wing meets down by the induced angle of attack.

Drag
: The part of the total aerodynamic force on a body that lies along the free-stream direction, opposing the motion. Both pressure and shear stress contribute to it.

Drag coefficient
: The dimensionless drag $C_D = D/(qS)$ of a wing or aircraft; for an airfoil, per unit span, $c_d = D'/(qc)$.

Drag loss
: The part of a launch vehicle's ideal velocity change that air drag takes away during the climb, $\Delta V_\text{drag} = \int (D/m)\,dt$ over the burn. It is added to the mission's delta-V when a stage is sized.

Drag polar
: The relation between the drag coefficient and the lift coefficient of an aircraft, a wing, or an airfoil section, for one configuration, Reynolds number, and Mach number. In its parabolic form, $C_D = C_{D,0} + K C_L^2$ with $K = 1/(\pi e\,\textit{AR})$.

Dynamic pressure
: $q = \tfrac12 \rho V^2$, in Pa: the kinetic energy of a flow per unit volume. A pitot-static tube measures it as the difference between the total and the static pressure.

Dynamic similarity
: The condition in which the ratios of the forces in two flows are the same, so that one flow is a scaled copy of the other. For a geometrically similar model at low speed it requires the same Reynolds number; at high speed also the same Mach number.

Eccentricity
: The number $e$ that sets the shape of a conic section: 0 for a circle, between 0 and 1 for an ellipse, 1 for a parabola, and more than 1 for a hyperbola. For an ellipse, $e = (r_a - r_p)/(r_a + r_p)$.

Effective angle of attack
: The angle $\alpha_\text{eff}$ between the local relative wind and the chord line of a finite wing, the angle its sections actually work at: $\alpha_\text{eff} = \alpha - \alpha_i$.

Effective exhaust velocity
: The thrust of a rocket engine divided by its propellant mass flow rate, $c = F/\dot m = V_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e/\dot m$: the exit velocity corrected for the pressure term. It equals the exit velocity when the nozzle is ideally expanded.

Electric propulsion
: Rocket propulsion that uses electric power to accelerate the propellant, as ion and Hall-effect thrusters do. It gives the highest specific impulse and a thrust of millinewtons, limited by the power available.

Elevator
: The moving surface hinged to the trailing edge of the horizontal stabilizer. It pitches the aircraft: trailing edge up, nose up.

Empennage
: The tail group of an aircraft: the horizontal and vertical stabilizers with the elevator and the rudder.

Empty weight
: $W_e$, the weight of the aircraft without crew, payload, or fuel: structure, engines, landing gear, systems, avionics, and furnishings.

Empty-weight fraction
: $W_e/W_0$, the empty weight divided by the takeoff weight. For a first estimate it comes from a fit to earlier aircraft of the same class, $W_e/W_0 = A\,W_0^{\,C}$ (Raymer, Table 3.1).

Endurance
: The time an aircraft stays in the air on its fuel. In sizing, the duration $E$ of a loiter segment, whose weight fraction is $W_i/W_{i-1} = \exp[-E\,C/(L/D)]$ with $E$ in hours.

Equation of state
: The relation $p = \rho R T$ between the pressure, density, and absolute temperature of an ideal gas, with $R$ the specific gas constant of the gas.

Escape speed
: The lowest speed at radius $r$ that carries a spacecraft away from the central body for good: $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2\mu/r}$, which puts it on a parabola.

Excess power
: The difference $P_A - P_R$ between power available from the engines and power required for steady level flight at a given speed. Dividing excess power by aircraft weight $mg$ gives the steady rate of climb.

Exit velocity
: The speed $V_e$ of the exhaust gas as it leaves the exit plane of a nozzle.

Finite wing
: A real wing, with tips. Air flows around the tips, so its lift coefficient and drag coefficient, written in capitals ($C_L$, $C_D$), differ from those of its airfoil.

Flap
: A moving surface on the trailing edge of the wing, between the fuselage and the aileron, that moves down (and often back) to add camber and area, so the wing makes enough lift at the low speeds of takeoff and landing.

Flight-path angle
: The angle $\gamma$ between the aircraft's velocity vector (flight path) and the horizontal, positive in a climb. In a steady climb, $\sin\gamma = (T_A - D)/(mg)$ and $R/C = V\sin\gamma$.

Flow separation
: The departure of a boundary layer from a surface, where a rising pressure has stopped the slow air near the wall. Behind it a wake forms; on a wing, separation causes the stall.

Free-stream velocity
: The velocity of the undisturbed air far ahead of a vehicle, relative to the vehicle, $V_\infty$. A vehicle moving through still air at a speed $V$ sees a free-stream velocity of the same size.

Fuel
: In a chemical rocket, the propellant that burns, such as kerosene, liquid hydrogen, or MMH.

Fuel fraction
: $W_f/W_0$, the weight of all the fuel loaded divided by the takeoff weight: $W_f/W_0 = 1.06\,(1 - W_x/W_0)$, where $W_x/W_0$ is the product of the segment weight fractions. It does not depend on the size of the aircraft.

Fuselage
: The central body of an aircraft. It holds the crew, passengers, and cargo, and joins the wing and the tail.

Geometric similarity
: The condition in which a model has the shape of the full-size object: every length scaled by the same factor and every angle the same.

Geostationary orbit
: A circular orbit in the equatorial plane with a period of one sidereal day. The spacecraft stays above one point on the equator, so a ground antenna can point at it without tracking.

Geosynchronous orbit
: An orbit whose period is one sidereal day. Its ground track repeats every day; a circular, equatorial one is a geostationary orbit.

Gradient layer
: A layer of a model atmosphere in which the temperature changes linearly with altitude, $T = T_1 + a\,(h - h_1)$, with a constant lapse rate $a$.

Gravitational parameter
: The product $\mu = GM$ of the universal gravitational constant and the mass of the central body, in km³/s². Earth's is $3.986 \times {10}^{5}$ km³/s².

Gravity loss
: The part of a launch vehicle's velocity change spent holding the vehicle up against gravity while it climbs, $g\sin\gamma$ for each second at flight-path angle $\gamma$.

Gross liftoff mass
: The mass of a launch vehicle on the pad at liftoff, with all its stages, propellant and payload: the initial mass of stage 1. Often abbreviated GLOW, for gross liftoff weight.

Ground loop
: A sudden swerve of an aircraft on the ground. A tail-dragger can fall into one because its center of gravity is behind the main wheels, so its weight keeps a turn going.

Ground track
: The path on a planet's surface directly below an orbiting spacecraft. On a planet that turns eastward, as Earth does, each pass crosses the equator farther west than the one before.

Hazard
: A condition of the operating environment that can harm a vehicle or its mission, such as icing, bird strikes, atomic oxygen, or radiation. It is described by its cause, its effect, and the mitigations that reduce it.

Heat of vaporization
: $h_\text{vap}$, the energy that turns one kilogram of a liquid at its boiling point into vapor, in J/kg (tables often give kJ/kg). About 446 kJ/kg for hydrogen and 213 kJ/kg for oxygen at atmospheric pressure.

Hohmann transfer
: A transfer between two circular orbits in the same plane along half of an ellipse that touches both, with one tangential burn at each end. Of all two-burn transfers between such orbits, it needs the least delta-V.

Hoop stress
: The tension that runs around the wall of a pressurized cylinder or sphere. In a thin wall of radius $r$ and thickness $t_\text{w}$ under the pressure $p$ it is $p\,r/t_\text{w}$ for a cylinder and half that for a sphere.

Hybrid rocket
: A rocket that keeps a solid fuel in its case and a liquid or gaseous oxidizer in a tank. It burns only while the oxidizer flows, so a valve starts and stops it.

Hydrostatic equation
: The force balance on a thin slab of fluid at rest, $dp = -\rho\,g_0\,dh$: the pressure falls with height by the weight of the fluid passed.

Hyperbolic excess speed
: The speed $v_\infty$ a spacecraft has relative to a planet when it is far from the planet, after climbing out of the planet's gravity well. On the hyperbola, $v^2 = v_\infty^2 + 2\mu/r$.

Hypergolic
: Said of a fuel and an oxidizer that ignite on contact, with no igniter, such as nitrogen tetroxide with MMH. Restarts are simple; a leak that mixes them is dangerous.

Ideally expanded
: The state of a nozzle whose exit pressure equals the ambient pressure, $p_e = p_a$: the pressure term of the thrust is zero and the jet leaves straight. It is the best nozzle for that one ambient pressure.

Impulsive burn
: A burn treated as instantaneous: the velocity changes at once and the position does not, so the new orbit passes through the burn point.

Inclination
: The angle $i$ between the orbit plane and the reference plane (the equator, for an Earth orbit), measured at the ascending node, from 0° to 180°.

Incompressible flow
: A flow in which the density is the same everywhere. Liquids, and air below about 100 m/s (Mach 0.3), are treated as incompressible.

Indicated airspeed
: The speed shown by an airspeed indicator, $\sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho_\text{SL}}$, computed with the sea-level standard density whatever the altitude. It equals the true airspeed only where the air has the sea-level density.

Induced angle of attack
: The angle $\alpha_i$ by which the downwash tilts the local relative wind below the free stream. For an elliptic lift distribution $\alpha_i = C_L/(\pi\,\textit{AR})$ in radians; for other wings, approximately $\alpha_i = 57.3\,C_L/(\pi e\,\textit{AR})$ in degrees.

Induced drag
: The drag a finite wing pays for its lift: the downwash tilts the lift back by the induced angle of attack, and the backward component is the induced drag. Its coefficient is $C_{D,i} = C_L^2/(\pi e\,\textit{AR})$, so it is zero when the wing makes no lift.

Inert mass
: Everything in a stage that is not propellant: the empty tanks, the engines, the pipes and the structure, written $m_\text{inert}$.

Inert mass fraction
: $f_\text{inert} = m_\text{inert}/(m_\text{inert} + m_\text{prop})$, the inert mass of a stage divided by its own loaded mass, inert mass plus propellant. The payload is not part of it. Real stages have about 0.057 to 0.23.

Infinite wing
: A wing of constant section with no tips, so the flow over it is two-dimensional. A wind tunnel model that spans the test section from wall to wall behaves as one; its coefficients, the airfoil data, are written in lowercase ($c_l$, $c_d$, $c_m$).

Interference drag
: The extra parasite drag where two parts of an aircraft meet, such as a wing and a fuselage: the drag of the pair is more than the sum of the two alone. A fairing in the corner reduces it.

Interstage
: The structure between two stages of a launch vehicle. It surrounds the engine of the upper stage and falls away with the lower stage.

Inviscid flow
: A flow taken to have no friction (no shear stress). Bernoulli's equation assumes it; close to a surface, in the thin layer where friction acts, a real flow is not inviscid.

Isothermal layer
: A layer of a model atmosphere in which the temperature is the same at every altitude. Pressure and density fall exponentially with height in it.

Kinematic viscosity
: The dynamic viscosity of a fluid divided by its density, $\nu = \mu/\rho$, in m²/s. For sea-level air it is $1.460 \times {10}^{-5}$ m²/s.

Knot
: The unit of speed of air navigation: one nautical mile per hour, 0.514 m/s.

Laminar flow
: Flow in which the fluid moves in smooth layers that slide over one another without mixing. A boundary layer is laminar near a leading edge and at low Reynolds numbers.

Lapse rate
: The rate of change of temperature with altitude in a gradient layer, $a = dT/dh$, in K/m. It is negative where the temperature falls with height: $a = -0.0065$ K/m in the troposphere of the standard atmosphere.

Launch azimuth
: The direction in which a launch vehicle flies, measured at the launch site in the horizontal plane, clockwise from north: ${90}^\circ$ is due east.

Launch vehicle
: A rocket that carries a payload from the ground into space, usually in stages that are dropped as their propellant runs out.

Launch window
: The span of time in which a launch can reach its target orbit. For a launch into a given orbit plane it falls around the moment the rotating launch site passes through that plane.

Lift
: The part of the total aerodynamic force on a body that is perpendicular to the free-stream direction. A wing produces it by turning the flow; both pressure and shear stress contribute to it.

Lift coefficient
: The dimensionless lift $C_L = L/(qS)$ of a wing or aircraft; for an airfoil, per unit span, $c_l = L'/(qc)$.

Lift-curve slope
: The slope of the straight part of the lift coefficient against the angle of attack, per degree: $a_0$ for an airfoil, $a = a_0/(1 + 57.3\,a_0/(\pi e\,\textit{AR}))$ for a finite wing.

Lift-to-drag ratio
: $L/D$, the lift of an aircraft divided by its drag: a measure of its aerodynamic efficiency. Jet airliners reach a maximum of about 15 to 21.

Likelihood
: How likely the event of a risk is to happen, scored in these notes from 1 (very unlikely) to 5 (very likely).

Line of nodes
: The line along which the orbit plane crosses the reference plane (the equator, for Earth orbits), or along which two orbit planes cross. It passes through the center of the central body; a plane change can only be made at a point on it.

Loiter
: A mission segment flown in circles at the speed that uses the least fuel per hour, while waiting to land or while watching an area. Airline pilots call it a hold.

Low Earth orbit
: An orbit around Earth with an altitude between about 160 km and 2,000 km. Below it, drag brings a satellite down within days.

Mach number
: The speed of a vehicle divided by the speed of sound in the air around it, $M = V/a_s$. Below 1 the flight is subsonic, above 1 supersonic.

Manometer
: A tube of liquid, often U-shaped, that measures a pressure difference by the height difference $\Delta h$ of the liquid in its two legs: $\Delta p = \rho_\ell\,g\,\Delta h$, with $\rho_\ell$ the density of the liquid.

Mass flow rate
: $\dot{m} = \rho A V$, the mass of fluid that crosses a cross section of area $A$ per second, in kg/s.

Mass ratio
: The initial mass of a rocket divided by its final mass, $m_i/m_f$. By the rocket equation it equals $e^{\Delta V/c}$.

Maximum lift coefficient
: $C_{L,\max}$, the highest lift coefficient a wing (or $c_{l,\max}$, an airfoil) reaches before it stalls. Flaps and slats raise it for takeoff and landing.

Mean aerodynamic chord
: The area-weighted average chord of a wing, $\bar{c}$; for a straight-tapered wing $\bar{c} = \tfrac{2}{3}\,c_r\,(1 + \lambda + \lambda^2)/(1 + \lambda)$. It is the reference length for the pitching moment of the wing.

Metric
: In a requirement, the quantity that is measured, such as the range with a full passenger load. A requirement is a metric and a value.

Microburst
: A small, intense downdraft that spreads out in all directions when it reaches the ground. An aircraft that crosses it close to the ground meets a headwind, then the downdraft, then a tailwind.

Minimum-drag speed
: The speed at which an aircraft in steady level flight has the least drag. It gives the lift coefficient $C_L^* = \sqrt{C_{D,0}/K}$, where the induced drag equals the parasite drag and $L/D$ is largest.

Minimum-power speed
: The true airspeed $V_{P_R,\min}$ at which power required in steady level flight is smallest: $V_{P_R,\min} = {3}^{-1/4}\,V_{T_R,\min} \approx 0.760\,V_{T_R,\min}$, where $C_L = \sqrt{3 C_{D,0}/K}$ and induced drag is three times parasite drag.

Mission profile
: A sketch of a mission as altitude against distance (or time), cut into segments such as takeoff, climb, cruise, descent, loiter, and landing, with the end of each segment numbered from 0 at the start of the takeoff.

Mission statement
: A short description of the capability a system will provide and the benefit it brings to its customer. It names no specific design.

Mitigation
: An action, taken or planned, that makes harm from a hazard or a risk less likely or less severe. It acts on the cause, on the hazard itself, or on the effect.

Mixture ratio
: The mass of oxidizer burned for each unit mass of fuel in a bipropellant engine, written O/F.

Momentum thrust
: The part of rocket thrust that comes from the momentum the exhaust carries away, $\dot m V_e$.

Monopropellant
: A single propellant that releases its energy without a second one, usually by decomposing over a catalyst, as hydrazine does.

Nautical mile
: The unit of distance of air and sea navigation, exactly 1,852 m; written nmi.

Need statement
: One sentence, written as an attribute of the system, that says what a stakeholder needs from it without saying how the system will provide it, and without "must," "shall," or "should."

No-slip condition
: The fact that a fluid touching a solid surface moves with the surface: on a body at rest the fluid speed at the wall is zero.

Nozzle
: The converging and diverging duct behind the combustion chamber of a rocket engine. It speeds the gas up to the speed of sound at the throat and beyond it in the diverging section, trading pressure and temperature for speed.

Nuclear thermal rocket
: A rocket that heats hydrogen in a nuclear reactor and expands it through a nozzle. It was tested on the ground in the NERVA program and has not flown.

Open-circuit wind tunnel
: A wind tunnel that draws air from the room, passes it once through the test section, and returns it to the room. Also called an Eiffel or open-return tunnel.

Operating empty weight
: The empty weight of an airliner as airlines and manufacturers publish it: the empty aircraft with its crew and the items needed to operate it, but no payload and no usable fuel.

Orbit decay
: The slow loss of altitude of a low orbit caused by atmospheric drag. It speeds up as the orbit sinks into denser air and ends in reentry.

Orbital period
: The time $T$ for one revolution of a closed orbit: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/\mu}$. It depends only on the semi-major axis.

Overexpanded
: The state of a nozzle whose exit pressure is below the ambient pressure, $p_e < p_a$: the nozzle expanded the gas too far. The pressure term of the thrust is negative and the outside pressure squeezes the plume inward.

Oxidizer
: In a chemical rocket, the propellant that supplies the oxygen (or another element) the fuel burns with, such as liquid oxygen or nitrogen tetroxide.

Parallel staging
: Staging in which several stages, usually solid boosters, are strapped around a central stage and burn beside it from liftoff, then drop away when they are empty.

Parasite drag
: The drag an aircraft has just by moving through the air, whether or not it makes lift: skin friction drag, pressure drag, and interference drag. Its coefficient at zero lift is $C_{D,0}$.

Parking orbit
: A low circular orbit where the launch vehicle leaves a spacecraft, which waits there until it starts a transfer to its working orbit.

Patched conics
: An approximate method for interplanetary trajectories: inside a planet's sphere of influence the spacecraft moves on a conic around the planet, outside it on a conic around the Sun, and the conics are joined at the edge of the sphere of influence.

Payload
: What a mission exists to carry: passengers and their bags, cargo, sensors, or weapons. It does not include the crew. For a rocket, the payload is the satellite or spacecraft it carries; for one stage, it is everything above that stage.

Payload fairing
: The light shell at the nose of a launch vehicle that shields the payload on the climb through the atmosphere. It is dropped once the air is thin.

Payload fraction
: The payload of a stage or a vehicle divided by its initial mass, $m_\text{pay}/m_i$. For one stage it falls to zero at $\Delta V = c\ln(1/f_\text{inert})$.

Periapsis
: The point of an orbit closest to the central body, at radius $r_p = a(1 - e)$. Around Earth it is called the perigee, around the Sun the perihelion.

Phase angle
: The angle by which the target planet must lead the departure planet, seen from the Sun, at the moment of departure, so that the target reaches the meeting point together with the spacecraft.

Phasing orbit
: An orbit with a slightly different period from that of the original orbit, flown for one or more laps so that a spacecraft arrives back at the starting point earlier or later and meets a target there.

Pitch
: Rotation of an aircraft about its $y$ body axis, the axis out along the right wing. Positive pitch raises the nose.

Pitching moment
: The moment of the aerodynamic forces about a point on the chord, which tends to raise or lower the nose; positive nose up. Its coefficient is $C_M = M/(qSc)$, or $c_m = M'/(qc^2)$ for an airfoil.

Pitot tube
: A tube that points into the flow with a small hole at its tip, where the flow stops, so the tube carries the total pressure $p_0$ to an instrument. A pitot-static tube adds static holes in its side.

Plane change
: A burn that turns the orbit plane by an angle $\Delta i$ while keeping the speed, costing $2v\sin(\Delta i/2)$.

Planform
: The shape of a wing seen from above.

Polar orbit
: An orbit with an inclination of ${90}^\circ$, or close to it, which passes over both poles.

Power available
: The propulsive power $P_A$ that an aircraft's engines deliver to the airframe at a given altitude, speed, and throttle setting: $P_A = T_A V$ for a jet, and $P_A = n\,\delta_t\,\eta\,P_0(\rho/\rho_0)^{m_{\text{ad}}}$ for a propeller aircraft.

Power required
: The propulsive power $P_R = T_R V = D V$ needed to hold steady level flight at true airspeed $V$, equal to $\tfrac12 \rho V^3 S C_{D,0} + 2K(mg)^2/(\rho S V)$.

Power specific fuel consumption
: The weight of fuel burned per unit shaft power per unit time by a propeller engine, $c$, with SI base units of $\text{N}/(\text{W}\cdot\text{s}) = 1/\text{m}$. Manufacturers often quote fuel mass per second per kilowatt, which multiplies by ${10}^{-6}\,g$ to convert to $1/\text{m}$.

Pressure drag
: The parasite drag from the difference between the high pressure on the front of a body and the low pressure in the wake behind it, where the flow has left the surface. Also called form drag; streamlining reduces it.

Pressure thrust
: The part of rocket thrust that comes from the difference between the exit pressure and the ambient pressure over the exit area, $(p_e - p_a)A_e$. It is positive, zero, or negative.

Pressure-fed
: Said of a liquid rocket engine whose propellants are pushed from the tanks into the combustion chamber by gas pressure, usually helium from a high-pressure bottle. It is simple, but its tanks must hold more than the chamber pressure, so they are heavy. Compare pump-fed engines, which use a turbopump.

Prograde orbit
: An orbit with an inclination below ${90}^\circ$, in which the satellite moves eastward, the same way as Earth turns.

Project risk
: A risk that comes from the work of building a vehicle (design, suppliers, tests, money, time) and not from its operating environment, such as a late engine or mass growth.

Propellant
: All the mass a rocket carries to throw out of its nozzle; in a chemical rocket, the fuel and the oxidizer.

Propellant mass
: The mass of fuel and oxidizer a burn uses, $m_\text{prop} = m_i - m_f$.

Propulsive efficiency
: The ratio of useful propulsive power $T V_\infty$ to the rate of kinetic energy added to the airstream. For an air-breathing jet with matched exit pressure and small fuel flow, $\eta_p = 2/(1 + V_e/V_\infty)$.

Ramjet
: A supersonic air-breathing engine with no rotating compressor or turbine: oncoming air is compressed only by slowing through shock waves in a contoured inlet before subsonic combustion and expansion through a nozzle. It produces zero static thrust and requires a booster to reach operating speed.

Range
: The distance an aircraft can fly on one load of fuel, usually given in nautical miles.

Rate of climb
: The vertical component of an aircraft's velocity, $R/C = V\sin\gamma = (P_A - P_R)/(mg)$, in m/s or ft/min.

Redundancy
: The use of more than one independent means to do the same job, so that one unit can fail without losing the function. Two independent units that each fail with probability $p$ both fail with probability $p^2$.

Requirement
: A statement of what a system must do, made of a metric (the quantity that is measured) and a value (how much is required), with its reason recorded beside it. A good set of requirements is complete, dependent (each one names a result, not a design), and verifiable.

Reserve fuel
: Fuel an aircraft carries beyond what the mission burns, for a diversion or a delay. In sizing it is counted together with trapped fuel (fuel that cannot be drawn from the tanks and lines) as 6% of the fuel burned.

Retrograde orbit
: An orbit with an inclination between ${90}^\circ$ and ${180}^\circ$, in which the satellite moves westward, against Earth's rotation.

Reynolds number
: The ratio of the inertial to the viscous forces in a flow, $\textit{Re} = \rho V L/\mu$, with $L$ a characteristic length that must be named (a chord, a body length, a diameter). Two geometrically similar flows with the same Reynolds number have the same pattern of boundary layers, transition, and separation.

Risk
: An uncertain event that would hurt a mission or a project, written as "If [event], then [consequence]" and described by its likelihood and its consequence.

Risk matrix
: A five by five grid with likelihood on one axis and consequence on the other, in which each risk is placed by its two scores. In these notes a risk is High when likelihood times consequence is 15 or more, Medium from 5 to 12, and Low at 4 or less.

Rocket equation
: $\Delta V = c\ln(m_i/m_f)$, with $c = I_\text{sp}\,g_0$: the velocity change of a rocket that burns from an initial mass $m_i$ to a final mass $m_f$, with no gravity and no drag (the ideal rocket equation). First published by Konstantin Tsiolkovsky in 1903.

Roll
: Rotation of an aircraft about its $x$ body axis, the axis forward through the nose. Positive roll lowers the right wing.

Rudder
: The moving surface hinged to the trailing edge of the vertical stabilizer. It yaws the aircraft.

Scramjet
: A supersonic-combustion ramjet designed for hypersonic flight (above about Mach 5), in which the air remains supersonic through the combustor to avoid the extreme temperatures caused by slowing hypersonic flow to subsonic speed.

Semi-major axis
: Half the longest diameter of an ellipse, $a = (r_p + r_a)/2$. It sets the size, the period, and the energy of an orbit. It is infinite for a parabola and negative for a hyperbola.

Sensitivity check
: A test of a decision: change one weight (or one score), rescale the other weights so that they still add up to 1, recompute the totals, and see whether the winner changes. The result is reported with the decision.

Serial staging
: Staging in which the stages are stacked one on top of another and fire one after the other, each lit after the stage below it is dropped.

Service ceiling
: The practical operational altitude limit of an aircraft, defined as the altitude where the maximum rate of climb drops to $0.5$ m/s (about $100$ ft/min).

Settling chamber
: The widest part of a wind tunnel, just ahead of the contraction, where the air moves slowly; station 1 in these notes. Its honeycomb straightens the flow, and its fine screens break up large eddies.

Shear stress
: The tangential force per unit area that a moving fluid exerts on a surface by friction, in Pa. With pressure, it is one of the two sources of every aerodynamic force.

Shock
: A thin zone in a gas flow where the gas slows down and its pressure jumps. The bright diamonds in a rocket plume are shocks.

Sidereal day
: The time a planet takes to turn once relative to the stars. For Earth it is 86,164 s, a little shorter than the 86,400 s solar day.

Single-event effect
: A malfunction of an electronic part caused by one energetic charged particle: a flipped memory bit (an upset), a latchup that draws a large current until the power is cycled, or a burnout that destroys the part.

Single-point failure
: One part of a system, in hardware, software, or a person's action, whose failure alone would lose the mission.

Skin friction drag
: The parasite drag from the shear stress of the air on the surface of a body. It grows with the wetted area and the roughness of the surface.

Slat
: A moving surface on the leading edge of the wing that moves forward and down, opening a slot, so the wing can fly at a steeper angle to the air before it stalls.

Solar cycle
: The rise and fall of the Sun's activity over about 11 years, measured for example by the F10.7 radio flux. Near solar maximum the upper atmosphere is denser, so drag on low satellites is higher.

Solar radiation pressure
: The small, steady push of sunlight on each square meter of a spacecraft. It is nearly the same at every altitude of an Earth orbit, so above about 1,000 km, where drag is weak, it usually turns a spacecraft more than drag does.

Solid rocket motor
: A rocket whose fuel and oxidizer are mixed in one solid grain cast inside the motor case. It gives high thrust from a compact package and, once lit, burns until the grain is gone.

South Atlantic Anomaly
: A region over South America and the southern Atlantic Ocean where Earth's magnetic field is weaker than elsewhere, so the inner radiation belt reaches down to the altitudes of low orbits.

Span efficiency factor
: The factor $e$ that measures how close the spanwise lift distribution of a wing is to an ellipse: 1 for the elliptic distribution, less than 1 for any other. Written for a whole aircraft it is often called the Oswald efficiency factor.

Specific fuel consumption
: $C$, the weight of fuel a jet engine burns per hour for each pound of thrust it makes, in 1/h. About 0.5 1/h in cruise for a high-bypass turbofan.

Specific gas constant
: The universal gas constant divided by the molar mass of a gas, $R = R_u/M$, in J/(kg K). For air $R = 287$ J/(kg K).

Specific impulse
: The impulse an engine delivers per unit weight of propellant burned, with the weight taken at standard gravity: $I_\text{sp} = F/(\dot m g_0) = c/g_0$, in seconds. It measures how well an engine uses its propellant.

Specific orbital energy
: The kinetic plus potential energy per unit mass, $\varepsilon = v^2/2 - \mu/r = -\mu/(2a)$, in km²/s². It is the same at every point of an orbit; its sign tells whether the orbit is closed.

Speed of sound
: The speed at which a small pressure disturbance travels through a gas, $a_s = \sqrt{\gamma R T}$. In an ideal gas it depends only on the temperature: 340.3 m/s at sea level in the standard atmosphere.

Sphere of influence
: The region around a planet inside which only the planet's gravity is counted in the motion of a spacecraft. Its radius is about $r(\mu_{\text{planet}}/\mu_{\text{Sun}})^{2/5}$, with $r$ the planet's distance from the Sun.

Spoiler
: A panel on top of the wing that rises into the flow to reduce lift and add drag: to descend without gaining speed, to help roll the aircraft, and on the ground after touchdown.

Stability
: The tendency of an aircraft to return to steady flight after a gust, with no action from the pilot. The horizontal stabilizer gives it in pitch and the vertical stabilizer in yaw.

Stage
: A complete rocket within a launch vehicle, with its own tanks, engines, and structure, dropped when its propellant is used up.

Staging
: Building a launch vehicle as a set of rockets, called stages, each dropped once its propellant is used, so later burns do not push empty tanks and engines.

Stagnation point
: A point where the flow is brought to rest against a body, such as the tip of a pitot tube. The pressure there is the total pressure of the flow.

Stakeholder
: A person or group affected by a system or interested in it: the customer who pays, the users and maintainers, the developers, the regulators, and the public. Stakeholders are the source of a system's needs and constraints.

Stall
: The sudden loss of lift when a wing meets the air at too steep an angle and the flow breaks away from its upper surface.

Stall speed
: $V_\text{stall}$, the lowest speed at which an aircraft can hold steady, level flight: the speed at which the wing, at its maximum lift coefficient, just carries the weight, $V_\text{stall} = \sqrt{2(W/S)/(\rho\,C_{L,\max})}$.

Standard
: A statement of one thing you can do by the end of the course, written as an "I can ..." sentence. Each standard has its own page in these notes.

Standard atmosphere
: An agreed model of temperature, pressure, and density as functions of altitude alone, representative of year-round midlatitude conditions, used as a common reference for aircraft performance and instrument calibration.

Standard gravity
: The fixed acceleration $g_0$ used to convert between specific impulse and effective exhaust velocity, 9.81 m/s² in these notes, on every planet and in orbit. It is not the local gravity.

Static port
: A small hole flush with the side of an aircraft or a probe, placed where the flow runs parallel to the surface, so that it senses the static pressure of the free stream.

Static pressure
: The normal force per unit area, $p = dF/dA$ in Pa, that the molecules of a fluid exert on a surface moving with the fluid. In still air it is the air pressure, in a flow it is what a static port senses, and it is the $p$ of the equation of state.

Steady flow
: A flow in which nothing at a fixed point changes with time: the pressure, density, temperature, and velocity at each point stay the same.

Steady level flight
: Flight at constant speed and constant altitude, with no acceleration: lift equals weight and thrust equals drag.

Storable propellant
: A liquid propellant that stays liquid over a reasonable range of temperature and pressure and can be kept in closed tanks for a year or more, such as nitrogen tetroxide, hydrazine, and MMH.

Stratosphere
: The layer of the atmosphere above the troposphere. In the standard atmosphere its lower part, from 11 to 25 km, is isothermal at 216.66 K, and its upper part warms with height.

Stream tube
: The bundle of streamlines that pass through a small closed curve. No fluid crosses its wall, so the continuity equation applies to it as to a pipe.

Streamline
: In steady flow, the path that every fluid element passing a given point follows. The velocity is tangent to it at each point, so no fluid crosses a streamline.

Sun-synchronous orbit
: A near-polar, slightly retrograde low Earth orbit whose plane is turned by Earth's equatorial bulge once a year, so the spacecraft passes over each place at the same local solar time.

Swath width
: The width of the strip of ground an instrument sees as the spacecraft passes over; $w = 2h\tan(\text{FOV}/2)$ for an instrument looking straight down with a narrow field of view.

Sweep
: The angle $\Lambda$ between a line along a wing, such as its leading edge or its quarter-chord line, and a line perpendicular to the centerline. The sweep usually quoted is that of the quarter-chord line.

Synodic period
: The time between two successive returns of the same alignment of two planets as seen from the Sun, $S = 1/|1/T_1 - 1/T_2|$. A launch opportunity between them comes once every synodic period.

System
: A set of people, products, and processes that work together as one whole to provide a capability that meets a stated need or objective. It is described by its function, its inputs and outputs, its boundary, and its form.

Takeoff weight
: $W_0$, the weight of an aircraft at the start of its takeoff run: the sum of the crew, payload, fuel, and empty weights, with all the fuel for the design mission. Also called the takeoff gross weight or the gross weight.

Taper ratio
: $\lambda = c_t/c_r$, the tip chord of a wing divided by its root chord: 1 for a rectangular wing, 0 for a pointed one.

Test section
: The narrowest part of a low-speed wind tunnel, where the air is fastest and the model is mounted; station 2 in these notes.

Throat
: The narrowest section of a rocket nozzle, where the gas reaches the speed of sound.

Thrust
: The force an engine exerts on its vehicle by throwing mass backward, $F$, in newtons. For a rocket it is $F = \dot m V_e + (p_e - p_a)A_e$.

Thrust available
: The thrust $T_A$ that an aircraft's engines can produce at a given altitude, speed, and throttle setting: $T_A = n\,\delta_t\,T_0(\rho/\rho_0)^{m_{\text{ad}}}$ for a jet, and $T_A = P_A/V$ for a propeller aircraft.

Thrust required
: The thrust $T_R$ needed to hold steady level flight at true airspeed $V$, equal to the drag $D = \tfrac12 \rho V^2 S C_{D,0} + 2K(mg)^2/(\rho S V^2) = mg/(L/D)$.

Thrust specific fuel consumption
: The weight of fuel burned per unit thrust per unit time by a jet engine, $c_t$, with SI base units of $1/\text{s}$ (often quoted in $1/\text{h}$; divide by $3{,}600$ to get $1/\text{s}$).

Thrust-to-weight ratio
: $T/W$, the thrust of the engines divided by the weight of the aircraft, which sets the size of the engines. A propeller aircraft uses the power-to-weight ratio $P/W$ instead. For a rocket it is written $F/W$, with the weight taken at standard gravity, $W = m g_0$.

Total ionizing dose
: The energy that charged particles leave in electronic parts over a mission, and the damage that builds up with it until the parts fail.

Total pressure
: $p_0 = p + \tfrac12 \rho V^2$, the pressure a flowing fluid reaches when it is brought to rest without friction. It is constant along a streamline in steady, incompressible, inviscid flow.

Traceability
: The link from each requirement to the need it comes from, and from each need to the requirements that answer it. It is checked in a traceability matrix, where every row (need) and every column (requirement) must hold a mark.

Trade study
: A comparison of alternative ways to meet the same requirements, which ranks them against criteria taken from those requirements and drops the less promising ones. It records what each choice gives up.

Trade-study log
: A record with one entry per design decision: the decision, the options considered, the criteria and data, the result, what would change it, and who decided.

Transfer orbit
: The orbit a spacecraft follows between two burns that take it from one orbit to another; for a Hohmann transfer, half of an ellipse.

Transition
: The change of a boundary layer from laminar to turbulent. On a smooth flat plate it begins near a local Reynolds number $\textit{Re}_x = 5 \times {10}^{5}$; roughness and an unsteady oncoming flow bring it earlier.

Trip strip
: A thin band of roughness (grit or small bumps) fixed near the nose or leading edge of a wind-tunnel model to force the boundary layer turbulent where it would be on the full-size aircraft.

Tropopause
: The top of the troposphere, where the temperature stops falling with height: 11 km and 216.66 K in the standard atmosphere of these notes. It is the lower boundary of the stratosphere, not a layer.

Troposphere
: The lowest layer of the atmosphere, from the surface to 11 km in the standard atmosphere, in which the temperature falls with height at 6.5 K per kilometer.

True airspeed
: The speed of an aircraft relative to the air around it, $\sqrt{2(p_0 - p)/\rho}$ with the density of the air at its altitude.

True anomaly
: The angle $\theta$ at the central body from periapsis to the spacecraft, measured in the direction of motion.

Turbofan
: A gas-turbine engine in which the turbine drives a large ducted front fan that accelerates cool bypass air around the hot core. High-bypass turbofans power airliners; low-bypass turbofans power supersonic fighters.

Turbojet
: A gas-turbine engine whose entire airflow passes through five stages in order: inlet (diffuser), compressor, combustor, turbine, and nozzle, producing thrust only from the high-speed hot core exhaust.

Turboprop
: A gas-turbine engine in which extra power-turbine stages drive a front propeller through a shaft and reduction gearbox, combining turbine power-to-weight with high propeller efficiency below about Mach $0.55$.

Turbopump
: A pump for each propellant driven by a turbine on the same shaft; it raises the pressure of the propellants between the tanks and the combustion chamber, so the tanks can stay at low pressure. The turbine is driven by hot gas from a small gas generator or a preburner.

Turboshaft
: A gas-turbine engine built to deliver nearly all of its energy as shaft torque, not jet exhaust, commonly used to drive helicopter rotors and auxiliary power units.

Turbulent flow
: Flow in which the fluid tumbles in eddies of many sizes that mix it across the layers. A turbulent boundary layer is thicker than a laminar one, has more skin friction, and stays attached longer against a rising pressure.

Turning vanes
: The rows of curved plates in the corners of a closed-circuit wind tunnel that guide the air around each bend without separating.

Two-body problem
: The motion of two point masses under their mutual gravity alone. Its solution is a conic section with the central body at a focus.

Ullage
: The gas space left on purpose above the liquid in a propellant tank, for the pressurizing gas and for the liquid to expand as it warms. Usually given as a percentage of the liquid volume.

Underexpanded
: The state of a nozzle whose exit pressure is above the ambient pressure, $p_e > p_a$: the nozzle did not expand the gas far enough. The pressure term of the thrust is positive and the plume spreads outward.

Validation
: Showing that a system meets its stakeholders' needs: that the right system was built.

Venturi
: A duct that narrows smoothly to a throat and widens again. The pressure difference between the inlet and the throat gives the speed of the flow, by continuity and Bernoulli's equation.

Verification
: Showing that a system meets its requirements, by analysis, inspection, demonstration, or test: that the system was built right.

Vertical speed indicator
: The flight instrument that shows the rate of climb or descent. It compares the static pressure inside a diaphragm with the pressure in its case, fed through a calibrated leak, and so senses how fast the static pressure is changing.

Vis-viva equation
: $v^2 = \mu(2/r - 1/a)$: the speed at radius $r$ on an orbit of semi-major axis $a$, for every conic section.

Viscosity
: The resistance of a fluid to the sliding of one layer over another: the dynamic viscosity $\mu$ in $\tau = \mu\, du/dy$, in kg/(m s). For a gas it rises with temperature; for a liquid it falls.

Volume flow rate
: $Q = A V$, the volume of fluid that crosses a cross section of area $A$ per second, in m³/s.

Wake
: The region of slow, often swirling fluid behind a body, downstream of where the flow has separated or the boundary layers leave the surface.

Wave drag
: The drag from shock waves that form on an aircraft as it nears the speed of sound. It rises sharply once the speed passes the critical Mach number.

Weight fraction
: $W_i/W_{i-1}$, the weight at the end of mission segment $i$ divided by the weight at its start. The fractions of all the segments multiply to give the weight at the end of the mission as a fraction of the takeoff weight.

Weight of a criterion
: A fraction that says how much a criterion counts in a decision matrix; the weights of all criteria add up to 1 and are set before any concept is scored. It is a pure number, not the weight of the vehicle.

Wind shear
: A sudden change of wind speed or direction over a short distance. Near the ground it can take away the airspeed of an aircraft that is taking off or landing.

Wind tunnel
: A facility that moves air past a model held still, so that the forces, pressures, and flow pattern of a flight can be measured on the ground.

Wing loading
: The takeoff weight divided by the wing area, $W_0/S$, in lb/ft² or N/m². The higher it is, the smaller the wing for the aircraft and the faster it must fly to make enough lift.

Winglet
: A small surface rising from a wing tip that lowers the induced drag of the wing in cruise.

Wingtip vortex
: The swirl of air that trails from each tip of a lifting wing, formed as air flows around the tip from the higher pressure below the wing to the lower pressure above. Seen from behind, the right-tip vortex turns counterclockwise and the left-tip vortex clockwise.

Yaw
: Rotation of an aircraft about its $z$ body axis, the axis pointing down. Positive yaw swings the nose to the right.

Zero-lift angle of attack
: The angle of attack $\alpha_{L=0}$ at which an airfoil or wing makes no lift: zero for a symmetric airfoil, negative for a positively cambered one. A finite wing has the same zero-lift angle as its airfoil.

Zero-lift drag coefficient
: $C_{D,0}$, the drag coefficient of an aircraft when it makes no lift. It uses the wing area as the reference area, although most of the drag comes from the fuselage, the tails, and the engine nacelles.
